## What does the app actually do each evening?

- **The Evening Examination.** Three prompts taken straight from the old practice: what you did well, what badly, what you left undone. Type it, or dictate it and edit before saving. You set the evening hour yourself, anywhere from 17:00 to 23:00.
- **He answers.** After you save, Aurelius replies with a reflection or a directive. If his answer ends on a question, you can reply in chat.
- **Your morning is in his hands.** The Morning Account, written before the day began, is what he measures the evening against.
- **What Must Be Done.** If you add this section, you name one to three things in the morning. In the evening he asks about each one, and you mark it Kept or Missed.
- **The Measure.** An optional 30-second score: Energy, Focus, Body and Satisfaction, 1 to 10 each.
- **The week.** A Weekly Lesson reads what you wrote that week and gives you one challenge for the next.

Today and yesterday can still be edited. From two days old a day is sealed, on purpose, so the record stays as you lived it.

## What is the Stoic evening review?

It is a look back at the day, every night, measured against your own principles. The practice is older than the Stoics.

The Golden Verses attributed to Pythagoras say: "Let not sleep fall upon thy eyes till thou hast thrice reviewed the transactions of the past day. Where have I turned aside from rectitude? What have I been doing? What have I left undone which I ought to have done?" Epictetus quotes these lines to his students (Discourses 3.10).

**Seneca's version** is the clearest. In On Anger (3.36) he says his teacher Sextius ended each day by asking himself which bad habit he had cured, which fault he had resisted and in what way he was better. Then he describes his own:

> "When the light has been removed and my wife has fallen silent, aware of this habit of mine, I examine my entire day and go back over what I've done and said, hiding nothing from myself and passing nothing by."

Three things stand out. He waits until the house is quiet. He hides nothing. And his tone is firm but not cruel: he forgives the day's fault and tells himself not to repeat it.

## Did Marcus Aurelius do an evening review?

Not in so many words. The Meditations are private notes Marcus wrote to himself, much of them while on campaign in the 170s. Their Greek title is usually given as "to himself". There is no record that he wrote them at night.

But they read like an examination. He returns to the same faults again and again: anger at difficult people, distraction, forgetting that he is mortal. That repetition is the point. The same tests come back tomorrow, and the review keeps you honest about them.

## Why do most people quit the evening review?

The practice is simple and hard to keep. The usual reasons:

- **Nobody reads it.** In a notebook you are the writer, the judge and the only reader. Most people acquit themselves.
- **Nothing compares.** You write "I should have been more patient" and never see that you wrote it last Tuesday too.
- **Honesty fades.** The first week is blunt. By the third, "today was okay" replaces the real answer.
- **It fades out.** You skip Friday, then the weekend, then Monday. Nobody notices.

Seneca and Marcus had teachers and a philosophical circle around them. Someone journaling alone at night has none of that. That is the gap the app is built for: a reader who remembers what you said this morning and last week.

## How do you do a five-minute evening review?

With or without an app:

1. **Read your morning.** What did you say you would do?
2. **What did I do well?** Something you did, not something that happened to you. Be specific.
3. **What did I do badly?** Name it, and the principle it broke. "I said no email before noon and checked it at 9:15 because I was bored" is honest. "I was a bit scattered" is not.
4. **What did I leave undone?** One thing, and when you will do it.
5. **Is this the same as last week?** If a fault keeps returning, name it.

Keep it short. The Meditations are often a few sentences at a time. Length gives you room to explain yourself. Brevity makes you say it.
