## The Short Answer

The complete Stoic daily framework takes ten minutes: five in the morning for preparation and intention, five in the evening for review and scoring. The morning practice sets a measurable standard. The evening practice audits against it. Neither practice reaches full power alone. Together, they create a closed loop of intention, action, and honest evaluation.

## The Morning Practice: Five Minutes That Set the Standard

Marcus Aurelius opens Book 2 of the Meditations with a morning reflection: "Begin each day by telling yourself: Today I shall be meeting with interference, ingratitude, insolence, disloyalty, ill-will, and selfishness." This isn't negativity. It's the Stoic practice of premeditatio malorum, the premeditation of difficulties, and it's the foundation of the morning framework.

The morning practice has two components. The first is rehearsal. The second is commitment.

**Component 1: Premeditatio Malorum (2 minutes)**

The rehearsal phase asks one question: what's likely to go wrong today, and how do you want to handle it?

This isn't a comprehensive risk assessment. It's a brief mental scan. Look at your calendar, your task list, your obligations. Identify the one or two moments most likely to test your composure. Maybe it's a difficult conversation you've been avoiding. Maybe it's the 3 PM energy crash where you typically reach for distractions. Maybe it's an interaction with someone who reliably irritates you.

The point isn't to predict the future. It's to pre-decide responses. Once you've rehearsed staying calm in the meeting, the meeting can't ambush you. The rehearsal doesn't prevent difficulty. It prevents surprise, and surprise is what triggers reactive behavior.

Seneca framed it this way: "Nothing happens to the wise man against his expectation." Not because the wise man predicts everything, but because he's rehearsed the range of what could happen and decided how to respond.

In practice, this is two minutes of mentally walking through the day ahead. Picture the hard parts. Decide what virtue each one demands. Patience for the conversation. Discipline for the afternoon slump. Courage for the email you need to send. The specificity matters. "I'll be virtuous today" is too vague to be useful. "I'll stay patient in the 2 PM call even if the scope changes again" is actionable.

**Component 2: Intention Setting (3 minutes)**

The intention phase answers: what is the one standard you're holding yourself to today?

This is where you write. Not much. Three to five sentences. A simple format:

*What's the most important thing today?* (One task, one outcome, one commitment.)

*What virtue does today demand most?* (Courage, discipline, justice, wisdom, patience.)

*What will I be tempted to avoid, and what does facing it look like?*

That last question is the sharp one. It forces you to name the avoidance before it happens. Most days, you know exactly what you'll try to skip. The hard call. The workout. The writing session. By naming the avoidance in the morning, you've made a contract with yourself. The evening review will ask whether you honored it.

The morning entry doesn't need to be long. Brevity forces clarity. If you can't state your intention in three sentences, you don't understand your own day well enough. The [Stoic journal prompts](/guides/stoic-journal-prompts/) that suit this phase are specific and confrontational, not inspirational quotes.

## The Evening Practice: Five Minutes That Close the Loop

Seneca described his evening practice in "On Anger": "When the light has been removed and my wife has fallen silent, aware of this habit that is now mine, I examine my entire day and go back over what I've done and said, hiding nothing from myself and passing nothing by."

This is one of the oldest recorded journaling practices in Western philosophy. It's also one of the most straightforward. Three questions, answered honestly.

**The Three Questions (2 minutes)**

1. What went well today, and what made it work?
2. What went poorly, and what was my role in it?
3. What did I leave undone that I should have done?

The first question isn't gratitude practice, though it overlaps. It's causal analysis. Something went well. Why? Was it preparation, discipline, skill, or luck? If it was luck, you don't get credit. If it was preparation, you can repeat it.

The second question is where most people flinch. "What went poorly" is easy. "What was my role in it" is hard. The natural instinct is to blame circumstances, other people, bad timing. The Stoic evening review strips that away. Your role. Your choices. Your response. Even if someone else created the situation, you chose how you responded to it. That response is the only part you own.

The third question catches the silent failures. Things that went poorly are visible. Things left undone are invisible. The workout skipped. The conversation postponed. The creative work replaced by busywork. These omissions don't announce themselves. The third question surfaces them.

**The Audit Against Morning Intention (1 minute)**

This is where the morning and evening practices connect. Reread your morning entry, typically just three sentences, and compare it to what actually happened.

Did you do the most important thing? Did you practice the virtue you committed to? Did you face the thing you predicted you'd avoid?

The answers are usually mixed. That's the point. The framework doesn't demand perfection. It demands awareness. You didn't stay patient in the call. Fine. You noticed, you named it, you'll try again tomorrow. The failure is data, not punishment.

Without the morning intention, this step doesn't exist. The evening review becomes a narrative of the day rather than an evaluation against a standard. Narratives are comfortable. Evaluations are productive. This is the key difference between [regular journaling and structured Stoic practice](/guides/ai-journaling-vs-regular/).

**The Score (2 minutes)**

Scoring is not part of the ancient practice. But it can turn the review from subjective narrative into something you can track. Four dimensions, each scored 1-10:

*Energy:* How was my physical and mental energy today?
*Focus:* Did I work on what mattered, or did I scatter?
*Body:* Did I move, eat well, sleep enough?
*Satisfaction:* At the end of the day, am I content with how I spent it?

The numbers aren't scientific. They're honest self-assessment. A Focus score of 4 means you know you scattered. A Body score of 8 means the workout happened and the diet held. Over time, these scores create trend lines that reveal what single-day reflection can't. More on this in the [reflection scoring guide](/guides/ai-reflection-scoring/).

Keep the scoring quick. Don't deliberate. First instinct. If you have to think hard about whether today was a 6 or a 7, it was probably a 6. Hesitation on self-scoring is often optimism trying to edge the number up.

## Why Both Practices Together Are More Than Either Alone

The morning practice without the evening review is aspiration without accountability. You set intentions with no mechanism for evaluating whether you honored them. Good intentions feel productive. They aren't, without follow-through.

The evening review without the morning practice is reflection without a standard. You review your day, but against what? Without a morning commitment, the evening review has nothing concrete to evaluate. It becomes journaling, not auditing. There's nothing wrong with journaling. But this framework is specifically designed to be an accountability system, and accountability requires a prior commitment.

Together, they form a closed loop. Morning: set the standard. Day: live. Evening: measure against the standard. The loop closes every 24 hours. Nothing slips through uncounted for more than a day.

This daily loop matches what [Marcus Aurelius's evening review](/guides/evening-review-marcus-aurelius/) looked like in spirit. The Meditations read less like a book than like the residue of a daily discipline: preparation for the day and examination of his own conduct, kept up over years. What survives as philosophy was originally a practice.

The effect usually becomes visible after a couple of weeks. Week one is clumsy. The morning entries are vague, the evening scores feel arbitrary. By week two, the entries sharpen. You know what specificity the evening review demands, so the morning intention gets more precise. The evening scores calibrate because you have comparison points. A 7 in Focus means something now because you know what a 4 and a 9 felt like.

Over the following weeks, patterns emerge. Maybe your Focus slips a day or two after your Body score drops. Maybe a skipped morning practice leads to a scattered next day. The framework starts generating its own insights, not from any single entry but from the accumulated record.

## The Weekly Layer: The Weekly Review

Daily practice catches moments. Weekly practice catches patterns. Add a third element once a week: a 10-minute review of the entire week.

This is where you look at seven days of scores, seven days of morning intentions, seven evening reviews, and ask: what's the story of this week?

Not a summary. A diagnosis. What pattern repeated? Where did the same failure show up three times? What worked consistently versus what worked once by accident?

The weekly review is where you catch the self-deceptions that daily reviews miss. A single day of low satisfaction is an event. Three days of low satisfaction is a pattern, and the pattern usually points to something you're not addressing. The weekly review names it.

This three-layer cadence, morning, evening and weekly, is the [full framework for journaling like Marcus Aurelius](/guides/journal-like-marcus-aurelius/) adapted for modern practice. The ancient sources don't describe a weekly review as such. But the principle of examining patterns across time is entirely Stoic. Epictetus asked his students to examine not just individual actions but habits. The weekly review is how habits become visible.

## Adding AI to Close the Gap

The framework works on paper. Plenty of people keep it in a notebook for years without any technology. But there's a gap that paper can't close easily: pattern recognition across long stretches of time.

After a few months of entries, there are patterns you can't see by flipping pages. A dip in Body that comes before a dip in Focus. The same weekday that keeps going badly. Morning intentions that get vaguer every time you travel. These patterns exist in the record but are hard to see without help.

This is where AI adds something paper can't. Not just better prompts, though [questions that follow your own thread](/guides/stoic-journal-prompts/) are valuable. The real addition is a reader with your record in hand. An AI that has read months of your entries can say: "Your energy has been lower this week than it was last month, and the drop started when you stopped mentioning your workouts." That observation could take an hour of page-flipping to find on your own, if you noticed it at all.

The AI doesn't replace the framework. The framework is about 2,000 years old and works fine without technology. But AI narrows the gap between what the framework captures and what you can actually see in your own record. It turns a good practice into a [system that compounds over time](/guides/ai-journaling-app-that-learns/).

## How Aurelius Approaches This

Aurelius, a Stoic journal for iPhone, is built around this framework. Not because it's novel, but because it has worked for two millennia and still works today.

The morning half is the Morning Account: what is on your mind, what waits, what could go wrong. It is a fixed set of questions, the same rehearsal every day. If you want the old premeditation in full, add The Pre-Mortem in Journal Sections: how tomorrow breaks, and the move that stops it. The next day it asks how it went: I held it off, It broke, or Never came.

The commitment half is What Must Be Done, off by default and added in Journal Sections: the one to three things the day fails without. If its reminder bell is on, an evening message in chat asks about each one, and you mark it Kept or Missed. When Aurelius names a commitment in chat, Hold me to it appears under it and adds it to the list.

The evening half is the Evening Examination: what you did well, what badly, what you left undone. After you save a Morning Account or an Evening Examination, Aurelius answers, honest before kind. [Philosophical accountability](/guides/philosophical-accountability/) means he says what he sees in what you wrote, not what feels comfortable. The Measure is the separate daily score: Energy, Focus, Body and Satisfaction, each 1 to 10, plus one optional thing to mend, in about thirty seconds.

The weekly layer is the Weekly Lesson. During The 30-Day Protocol it lands on day 6, then about every seven days; after that, Sunday at 19:00 your time. It gives your AURA Level (0 to 100), your profile and the story of your week, and ends with one challenge for the week ahead that you accept and sign. The next Lesson asks whether you met it. Habits that keep returning become constellations in your Archive once they show on two separate days at least a week apart, and are proven at five. That's the [evening review tradition of Marcus Aurelius](/guides/evening-review-marcus-aurelius/) made practical with modern tools.
